# Sell the decision, not the calculator

No. Charging for the retirement and Coast FIRE calculators or the cash-flow forecast is not worth testing, whether as a $5 week pass, pay-what-you-want or a credit budget. The trial's last day is a better use of the same effort. The market expects these calculators to be free and instant. Every dedicated FIRE and Coast FIRE calculator checked (FIRECalc, cFIREsim, FI Calc, Engaging Data, WalletBurst) shows its answer with no signup. Paid planners charge **$80–$289 a year, almost always billed annually**, for saved plans, tax and Roth modelling and scenario comparison, never for the headline number. No one in retirement planning, FIRE or consumer budgeting sells a time-boxed pass or AI credits, and the closest attempt, Ahrefs' $7 seven-day trial, was shut down because people abused it. Ask Linc already asks more for a calculator result than any competitor does: an email address and a password, for a number that costs almost nothing to compute. The research on zero prices finds that crossing from free to *any* price destroys demand, and the size of the price matters much less. The arithmetic is harsh too: a $5 sale nets about $4.56 after Stripe fees, so one subscriber kept six months at $19 a month is worth about 25 of them. The forecast can't be sold as a one-time product at all, because it doesn't exist until someone signs up and links at least 28 days of transactions through Plaid, which bills per linked account (Item) per month. The pricing question that matters sits at day 30. The first automatic no-card trials end around **November 5, 2026**. Converting one depends on adding a card in the Stripe portal, a path nobody has confirmed against real Stripe. And Ask Linc has no annual plan in a market that bills annually. Fix and measure that first. Then test an annual price, and a pass that never renews offered to trial users who turn down the subscription.

## Every Coast FIRE competitor shows the number for free

In this category the calculation is free and the continuity is what costs money. The table below shows the pattern. Free calculators give the full result with no gate. Paid planners give away more each year: ProjectionLab's free tier includes Monte Carlo and historical backtesting, and tax estimation became free in October 2025 ([ProjectionLab changelog](https://projectionlab.com/changelog)). What they charge for is **saving and revisiting a plan**, plus tax, Roth, withdrawal and comparison tools ([ProjectionLab pricing](https://projectionlab.com/pricing); [Rob Berger](https://robberger.com/projectionlab-review/)).

One-time pricing survives only at the hobbyist edge: spreadsheets, a $9.99 app unlock, and Retirology's pay-what-you-want launch, which drew zero upvotes. A search aimed at "30-day pass" or "one-time access" retirement software found **no product selling time-limited access**, only trials, licences and flat-fee advisers ([Kiplinger](https://www.kiplinger.com/personal-finance/diy-financial-plan-tools); [CNBC Select](https://www.cnbc.com/select/best-retirement-planning-tools/)). The market leader's history matters here. ProjectionLab grew from $150 MRR in May 2021 to **$1M ARR in June 2025** without funding, and non-recurring revenue from Lifetime plans and 1-on-1 sessions ran **20–50% above MRR** ([ProjectionLab](https://projectionlab.com/blog/we-reached-1m-arr-with-zero-funding)). It still dropped the Lifetime option by July 2026 ([Rob Berger](https://robberger.com/projectionlab-review/)). Some of this audience clearly likes paying once. But the company that knows that audience best judged recurring billing worth more, and while it did sell a one-time option, that option was a plan for the full product, never a toll on its free calculator.

The other model, monetising calculator traffic as adviser leads, doesn't fit Ask Linc either. Empower pays referring sites **up to $250 a lead** ([Empower](https://www.empower.com/retire)). NerdWallet's adviser arm pays nothing at all for users with **under $100,000** in investable assets ([NerdWallet disclosure](https://cdns3.nerdwallet.com/nerdwallet-advisors/disclosures/promoter_solicitor_20250902.pdf)). Coast FIRE skews toward younger savers who are still accumulating, so most of that audience would fall below the threshold. Selling leads to advisers would also compromise Ask Linc's position as a neutral assistant.

Against this backdrop, the price Ask Linc already charges stands out. Since October 3 neither calculator shows its result on the page. The visitor gets a locked card, gives an email address, and chooses a password to see the answer inside Ask Linc ([COAST_FIRE_EMAIL_CAPTURE.md](../docs/COAST_FIRE_EMAIL_CAPTURE.md); [RETIREMENT_QUICKPLAN.md](../docs/RETIREMENT_QUICKPLAN.md)). That is stricter than every free competitor checked, and it is four days old, so there is no data on what it costs in completed runs. On these pages the real pricing risk is the friction already in place, not missing revenue.

## Forecasting sells inside subscriptions, at $4–$10 a month

The cash-flow market prices the forecast as a feature of a subscription, not as a product of its own. Mainstream budgeting apps settle at **$95–$109 a year or $13–$15 a month**: Copilot $95/yr ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/copilot-track-budget-money/id1447330651)), Monarch Core $99.99/yr ([Monarch](https://www.monarch.com/pricing)), YNAB $109/yr with a 34-day no-card trial ([YNAB](https://www.ynab.com/pricing)). Where forecasting is offered, it goes in one of three places. It can be in the only plan: Quicken Simplifi includes projected balances and what-ifs at $3.99–$6.99 a month billed annually ([Quicken](https://www.quicken.com/simplifi)). It can be gated by horizon: PocketSmith gives 6 months free, then 10, 30 or 60 years on paid tiers ([PocketSmith](https://my.pocketsmith.com/plans)). Or it can sit in a premium tier: Monarch Plus, launched April 2026, puts forecasting in a **$299/yr** plan for new members ([PR Newswire via Yahoo](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/articles/monarch-launches-premium-tier-monarch-124800073.html)). Standalone daily-balance forecasters such as Centinel charge $7.99/mo or $69.99/yr ([Centinel](https://www.centinelmoney.com/resources/centinel-vs-monarch-money)). Ask Linc's forecast covers recurring streams, a daily rate, planned events and card payoff. Functionally it sits closest to Simplifi, PocketSmith and Centinel, which the market prices at roughly $4–$10 a month inside a subscription.

No major US budgeting app sells a pass that doesn't renew or a credit pack. The closest precedents are Kubera's $1 trial, which is non-refundable and does not auto-convert ([Kubera help](https://help.kubera.com/article/117-why-pay-1-for-the-trial)), PocketGuard's $149.99 lifetime licence ([getfinny](https://getfinny.app/blog/pocketguard-pricing-2026)), and small iOS apps on auto-renewing $4.99 weekly plans ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6748604085)). Meanwhile the price of "ask an AI about my linked accounts" is falling toward zero. ChatGPT's Plaid-linked Finances feature launched for Pro in May 2026 ([Plaid](https://plaid.com/blog/chatgpt-personal-finance-plaid/)), and secondary reposts of OpenAI's release notes say it reached Free and Go users in the US on October 2 ([Releasebot](https://releasebot.io/updates/openai/chatgpt)). The first-hand page could not be fetched, so that date is unverified.

Ask Linc's own setup rules out a one-time forecast in any case. `/cash-flow-forecast` is a marketing page with no tool on it. It offers a trial signup and a sample forecast by email, and the sample form stores nothing in the app ([page](../frontend/src/app/cash-flow-forecast/page.tsx); [route](../src/routes/cash-flow-forecast.ts)). The real forecast needs a signed-in user and **at least 28 days of transaction history**, unless both income and spending are overridden by hand. Detecting monthly bills takes about three months of history ([CASH_FLOW_FORECAST.md](../docs/CASH_FLOW_FORECAST.md)). Plaid Transactions is billed **per connected Item per month** ([transaction-history.ts](../src/config/transaction-history.ts)). A "$5 forecast" would therefore ask a stranger to pay before seeing anything personal, then to link a bank, which leaves Ask Linc with a recurring Plaid cost for a one-off fee. The forecast only exists for someone who has signed up and linked an account, which makes it a subscription product.

## Crossing zero costs more than $5 can earn back

The behavioural evidence says the first cent hurts most. In Shampanier, Mazar and Ariely's real-purchase experiments, a Hershey's Kiss took **27% of choices at 1¢ and 69% at 0¢**, while cutting a positive price by the same cent made no measurable difference ([Zero as a Special Price](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf)). In the same paper, Amazon's free shipping lifted orders sharply everywhere except France, where a pricing error left shipping at about 10¢. Field evidence points the same way. A publisher's paywall cut visits by **51%** ([Chiou & Tucker](https://www.oxy.edu/sites/default/files/assets/Economics/Chiou/chiou_and_tucker_paywalls.pdf)). Of Blendle's 1M+ registered users, only 150,000 ever made a micropayment, and Blendle gave up on micropayments for a subscription ([Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2019/06/micropayments-for-news-pioneer-blendle-is-pivoting-from-micropayments/)). The nearest measure of putting a payment step in front of a signup comes from SaaS: organic visitors start no-card trials at **8.5%** but card-required trials at **2.5%** ([Shno, citing First Page Sage](https://www.shno.co/marketing-statistics/free-trial-conversion-statistics)). No study measures what happens when a personal-finance calculator moves from free to paid. Every analogue, though, points to losing well over half of completed captures.

The economics of a $5 charge don't make up for that loss. Stripe takes 2.9% + 30¢, so a domestic $5 sale nets **$4.56**. Refunds don't return the fee, and a lost dispute costs $15, which is the net from about 3.4 sales ([Stripe pricing](https://stripe.com/pricing)). A cheap checkout open to anonymous visitors from search and ads is exactly what card testers look for, according to Stripe ([Stripe Docs](https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/prevention/card-testing)). The damage would land on the same Stripe account that bills the subscription, just as Visa tightens its "excessive" fraud-and-dispute threshold to **1.5%** from April 2026 ([Justt](https://justt.ai/blog/visa-vamp-threshold-changes-april-2026/)). The comparison that settles it is with the subscription itself. At the code's $19 fallback price ([stripe-pricing.ts](../src/config/stripe-pricing.ts)), a subscriber who stays six months, an illustrative assumption since Ask Linc has no retention data yet, is worth $114, or **about 25 net $5 unlocks**. A gate that turns away a single eventual subscriber has to sell two dozen passes just to break even.

None of the three proposed models has a precedent that fits a free calculator used once:

| Model | Closest precedent | What happened | Fit for a calculator or forecast |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~$5 one-week pass | Ahrefs $7/7-day paid trial; auto-renewing weekly app plans | Ahrefs killed it after abuse ([Latka transcript](https://getlatka.com/interviews/ahrefs-tim-soulo-2024)); only **3.4%** of weekly subscribers remain after a year ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/weekly-subscriptions/)); weekly plans carry the "fleeceware" label ([Gen Digital](https://newsroom.gendigital.com/2021-03-24-Free-trial-for-Only-3,432-per-Year-Avast-Discovers-new-Fleeceware-Apps-on-the-Google-Play-and-Apple-App-Stores)) | Poor. The value is delivered in one session, a 7-day pass undercuts a 30-day trial that is already free, and $5 a week annualises to $260, above every planner's annual price except WealthTrace's top tier |
| Pay-what-you-want | Lunch Money's annual plan; *In Rainbows*; theme-park photos | Lunch Money (founder-reported, confounded by Mint's shutdown): **37% pay over $100, bringing in over 60% of revenue**, with the floor raised $40 → $50 → $60 ([Lunch Money](https://lunchmoney.app/blog/we-lowered-our-pricing); [2026 update](https://lunchmoney.app/blog/an-update-to-our-annual-plan-minimum-effective-march-15-2026)); **62%** of *In Rainbows* downloaders paid nothing ([comScore](https://www.comscore.com/Insights/Press-Releases/2007/11/Radiohead-Downloads)); PWYW photo buyers paid **$0.92** on average ([Freakonomics, on Gneezy et al.](https://freakonomics.com/2010/07/how-to-maximize-pay-what-you-wish-pricing)) | Poor for a one-shot tool used anonymously. It works on a subscription with a floor and price anchors |
| Credits or points | Cursor, Lovable, Gamma | Cursor refunded unexpected charges after its July 2025 switch ([FinTech Weekly](https://www.fintechweekly.com/magazine/articles/cursor-pricing-change-user-backlash-refund)); people prefer flat rates, and a bias toward pay-per-use raises churn ([Lambrecht & Skiera](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/marketing/paying_too_much.pdf)) | None. Credits exist to pass variable compute cost to the user, and these engines are deterministic and nearly free per run |

The case for charging deserves a fair hearing. FIRE audiences skew wealthy and financially literate ([WCI 2024 survey](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/2024-wci-annual-survey-results/)), so many could pay $5. Retirement planning happens in episodes: 80% of workers in a 2016 Voya survey had not reviewed their plan in a year ([BenefitsPRO](https://benefitspro.com/2016/12/13/waiting-in-line-at-the-dmv-less-painful-than-retir)). Subscription fatigue is real: 55% of Americans want to cut subscription spending in 2026 ([KPTV, on a NerdWallet survey](https://www.kptv.com/2026/04/07/more-than-half-americans-plan-cut-subscriptions-2026-survey-finds/)). Paid trials screen out people with no intent to buy. Each point is valid, but each supports a one-time product sold to people who have already seen their own numbers, not a toll on the free part of the funnel. Trust adds to the problem. For creating a retirement plan, **56% of Americans prefer a human and 13% prefer AI** ([Northwestern Mutual 2025](https://news.northwesternmutual.com/2025-08-05-Human-Connection-Over-Machines-Americans-Trust-Advisors-More-Than-AI-for-Financial-Advice,-Finds-Northwestern-Mutuals-2025-Planning-Progress-Study)), and 80% said in 2023 that they would not pay for AI retirement or finance insights ([Ipsos](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/one-five-investors-report-using-ai-chatbot-past-year)). A paid AI result shown before any relationship exists is the hardest version of this sale. A paywall would also contradict the documented beachhead thesis, in which a calculator result "creates demand for a plan using actual finances" ([MARKETING_DASHBOARD.md](../docs/MARKETING_DASHBOARD.md)). It would also need new engineering: the current gate is "a nudge, not a control", because the result already reaches the browser ([COAST_FIRE_EMAIL_CAPTURE.md](../docs/COAST_FIRE_EMAIL_CAPTURE.md)).

## The money is at day 30, and the conversion path there is untested

Ask Linc's revenue depends on one step that hasn't yet been tested. Since October 6, every no-card signup becomes a 30-day Stripe trial set to `missing_payment_method: cancel`, so nothing bills automatically. To keep access, a user has to add a card through the billing portal. The repo's own docs say it is **"still unconfirmed against a real Stripe account"** that this converts the trial rather than letting it cancel ([ADMIN_TRIALS.md](../docs/admin/ADMIN_TRIALS.md)). The trial-ending webhook handler still carries a `TODO: Send notification to user about trial ending`, which leaves the reminders to MailerLite ([stripe.ts](../src/services/stripe.ts)). Trials that need no card convert at roughly **18–25%** in SaaS benchmarks, against about 49–60% for card-required trials ([Shno](https://www.shno.co/marketing-statistics/free-trial-conversion-statistics); [Visionary Marketing](https://visionary-marketing.co.uk/blog/saas-free-trial-conversion-statistics-2026)). If the portal step fails, that rate is zero. The first cohort reaches day 30 around November 5. Verifying this path is worth more than any calculator pricing test.

The clearest gap against the market is billing period, not price model. Leading planners bill annually and budgeting apps push annual plans hard. Ask Linc offers only monthly billing, and every price resolves to a single `STRIPE_PRICE_DEFAULT` ([stripe-pricing.ts](../src/config/stripe-pricing.ts)). Twelve months at $19 is $228. That is above Boldin's new $168, ProjectionLab's $129 and Monarch Core's $99.99. Budgeting apps typically discount annual plans by 33–44% against monthly (YNAB $14.99 vs $109, Monarch $14.99 vs $99.99, PocketSmith's "save 33%"). Applying that discount to $19 a month gives roughly **$125–$150 a year**, which lands on the planner anchors. An annual option is the most market-standard pricing test available, and it has the strongest evidence behind it.

The founder's one-time instinct does have a defensible form: a **non-renewing pass offered only to trial users who decline the subscription**. It serves the episodic retirement user and the subscription-weary user at the moment they have seen their own data, which is where the evidence says payment intent lives. Buyers pay a premium for short commitments; weekly app subscribers pay **3.23× more per month** than monthly ones ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/weekly-subscriptions/)). The closest product, My Plan Keeper's AI retirement "Clarity Sprint", charges **$49 for 30 days**, though its page is undated ([My Plan Keeper](https://myplankeeper.com/)). Single financial deliverables (a state tax return, a vehicle history report) sell at **$15–$50** ([Toolradar](https://toolradar.com/tools/freetaxusa/pricing); [VinAudit](https://www.vinaudit.com/?p=11530)). Those anchors point to roughly $29–$49 for 30 days that never renew, priced above the monthly rate so the pass never undercuts the subscription. Kubera's non-converting trial shows that "this will not renew" can be stated plainly and trusted. Because the buyer is signed in, card testing is largely off the table. Building it means a Checkout session in `mode: 'payment'`, a `checkout.session.completed` handler and somewhere to store the entitlement, none of which exist today. It also means a guard on `getTierFromPriceId`, which maps every `price_…` ID to premium ([stripe.ts](../src/services/stripe.ts); [config](../src/config/stripe.ts)). The pass's expiry must land in a state that stops nightly Plaid refreshes, as `canceled` already does ([refresh eligibility](../src/services/subscription-refresh-eligibility.ts)).

The other two ideas have narrower uses. Pay-what-you-want works, on Lunch Money's evidence, as a variant of an annual subscription with a floor and visible anchors. That would be a later experiment, and one that needs Ask Linc's per-user LLM and Plaid costs first so the floor isn't set below cost; the repo has no dollar cost figures. Credits belong only as a free allowance in plain units, in the spirit of the existing three-run limit, never as a currency people buy.

## A test plan sized for a funnel with no A/B framework

Ask Linc can measure a pricing test, since first-party attribution, `signupOrigin`, cohort reports with a Paid toggle and a GA4 purchase event already exist. What it lacks is a way to assign visitors to variants at random ([COHORT_REPORTS.md](../docs/admin/COHORT_REPORTS.md); [schema](../prisma/schema.prisma)). The plan below works within that. It uses cohort alternation and the existing `UserAcquisition.flowVersion` field as the variant label, and never puts a lead at risk to answer a question the evidence already answers.

| Phase | When | What | Primary measure | Decide |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Plumbing and baseline | Now → Nov 5 | Confirm in Stripe test mode that a portal card converts a granted trial; ship the trial-ending notification; pull run → email → signup → link → paid by `signupOrigin` from the admin reports | Portal path works; baseline rates exist | Nothing else starts until both are true |
| 1. Willingness-to-pay survey | Oct–Nov | Van Westendorp plus Gabor-Granger questions on monthly, annual and a 30-day pass, sent to trial users and calculator leads through MailerLite; 200–300 completes | Acceptable price ranges by segment | Sets the annual and pass prices for phase 2 |
| 2. Trial-end offer test | From the first expiries, in whole weeks | Alternate signup-week cohorts: A monthly only; B monthly + annual; C monthly + annual, with the pass shown only after a decline | Revenue per trial starter at 60 and 90 days; paid rate; pass share; refunds and disputes | Keep the arm with the highest revenue per starter, unless subscriptions fall |
| Optional calculator painted door | Only to settle the question with Ask Linc's own data | Two-week split: "Unlock for $5" vs the current free gate; an honest reveal page delivers the result free; never collect a card | Email-capture rate | Expect a large drop; don't build a paywall |

Sample sizes, from a standard two-proportion power calculation (α = 0.05, 80% power), set realistic expectations. Detecting email capture halving from 20% to 10% takes about **200 visitors per arm**, so the optional painted door is cheap. Telling a 5% payer rate from 3% takes about **1,500 per arm**, and 3% from 2% about **3,800**. The Coast FIRE funnel runs on **$20 a day** of Google Ads ([PRODUCT_MILESTONES.md](../docs/admin/PRODUCT_MILESTONES.md)), so phase 2 will detect only large differences unless organic volume is much higher than paid. Check GA4 run counts before committing to arm sizes. Cohort alternation is vulnerable to seasonality and campaign changes. Freeze ad settings during the test, alternate weekly rather than running A, then B, then C in blocks, and don't stop early on a spike. Paid conversion can't be read until the 30-day trial and one billing cycle have passed.

## Conclusion

The question arrived as "how should we charge for these tools?" The evidence turns it into "where should the price sit?" The calculators and the forecast exist to produce trial starts with real data linked. Their output is a commodity that competitors give away. The linked-data follow-up is not, and Ask Linc's own design already makes "linking the upgrade rather than the price of an answer" ([SCENARIO_MODELING.md](../docs/SCENARIO_MODELING.md)). One-time revenue is real in this audience, at 20–50% on top of ProjectionLab's MRR. But it comes from people who already use the product, which is why the pass belongs at the end of the trial and not in front of the result. With general-purpose AI now giving away linked-account Q&A, Ask Linc's paid story has to rest on named deliverables: a stress-tested retirement decision, the forecast's low point, a card payoff plan. It should be sold when users see those deliverables computed from their own numbers.

Two unknowns could change this advice. Ask Linc's own funnel rates aren't in the repo, and no published study measures a finance calculator moving from free to paid. One result would reopen the calculator question: a baseline showing heavy run volume with almost no signups from it. That would mean the traffic doesn't value the product enough to justify the gate, let alone a price. Even then, the evidence points to less friction on the calculator and a paid offer after capture, not a charge for the number.
