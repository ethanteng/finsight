# Evidence on alternative pricing models for low-ticket digital tools (time-boxed passes, PWYW, credits, ~$5 micro-charges) vs free lead magnets and subscriptions

Context for the reader: Ask Linc's retirement/Coast FIRE calculators and the cash-flow forecast are free lead magnets today. The calculators already withhold the result behind an email form (a "locked card"), and that email goes on to signup and a 30-day no-card trial (see `CLAUDE.md` / `docs/COAST_FIRE_EMAIL_CAPTURE.md` in the repo). So the visitor already "pays" with an email address. Charging $5, PWYW or credits would put a payment step on top of, or in place of, that email gate. Unless marked otherwise, the dollar figures and sample sizes under "Inferences" are my own calculations from the cited inputs.

## 1. Zero-price effect and mental transaction costs: how much demand is lost going from free to even a small price?

### Takeaway
Demand at $0 is much higher than at any small positive price, and the jump is not smooth. In real-purchase experiments, a 1¢ cut that reaches zero moves choice shares by about 40–50 percentage points. The same 1¢ cut between two positive prices does nothing measurable. Real-world free-to-fee switches in digital media cut traffic by roughly half (one paywall field test) and left most users never paying (Blendle). I found no clean, published "free → $1–$5" drop-off figure for a web tool. The analogs suggest that crossing zero matters more than whether the price is $1 or $5.

### Cited Findings
- **Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely (2007), *Marketing Science* 26(6):742–757. Experiment 2 (real purchases).** Excluding people who chose nothing, the Hershey's Kiss share rose from **27% at 1¢ (with Lindt at 15¢) to 69% at 0¢ (with Lindt at 14¢)**. Lindt fell from 73% to 31%. Even when Lindt was cut 5¢ to 10¢ alongside a free Kiss (0¢ & 10¢), the split was 64% / 36%. The free condition still dominated even though the premium good got the bigger discount. — [Zero as a Special Price (PDF)](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf)
- **Same paper, Experiment 1.** Moving from 2¢ & 27¢ to 1¢ & 26¢ made an "imperceptible" difference in demand. Moving from 1¢ & 26¢ to 0¢ & 25¢ shifted demand substantially. The authors' reading: "when all prices are positive, a 1¢ change in prices does not have a significant effect on demand. Only when one of the prices becomes zero does the observed perturbation take place." — [Zero as a Special Price (PDF)](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf)
- **Same paper, Experiment 3 (cafeteria, transaction costs held constant).** Hershey's went from 21% (1¢ & 14¢) to 71% (0¢ & 13¢), and Lindt from 79% to 29%. The authors conclude the zero-price effect "is not produced solely by a difference in transaction costs." — [Zero as a Special Price (PDF)](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf)
- **Same paper, Amazon gift certificate follow-up** ($10 vs $20 certificates priced $5 & $12, $1 & $8, or $0 & $7). Demand did not differ between $5/$12 and $1/$8. At $0/$7, demand for the $10 certificate "rockets" and demand for the $20 certificate "falls to zero." — [Zero as a Special Price (PDF)](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf)
- **Mechanism.** The authors tested social norms, mapping difficulty and affect: "Affect emerges as the most likely account." In a −1¢ condition (free chocolate plus a penny), Hershey's rose further from 34% to 50% while Lindt stayed at 16%. So it is the price change itself that drives the shift, not the absence of a monetary frame. — [Zero as a Special Price (PDF)](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf)
- **Real-world anecdote reported in the paper (not a controlled test).** When Amazon introduced free shipping in some European countries, France was mistakenly priced at 1 franc (about 10¢). Orders "increased dramatically" in the free-shipping countries, but "not much change occurred in France." — [Zero as a Special Price (PDF)](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf)
- **Szabo (1999), "Micropayments and Mental Transaction Costs."** Szabo argues that the customer's "hassle factor" (unpredictable spending, the cost of learning what a product is worth, and the cost of deciding) usually exceeds and often dwarfs the computational costs of a payment. That puts a floor under how finely things can be priced. (I only had access to excerpts and secondary summaries.) — [Szabo paper (PDF mirror)](https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/rob/Courses/InformationInSpeech/CDROM/Literature/LOTwinterschool2006/szabo.best.vwh.net/berlinmentalmicro.pdf); [Hacking Distributed summary (2014)](https://hackingdistributed.com/2014/12/31/costs-of-micropayments/); [Bitcoin Magazine 25-year retrospective](https://bitcoinmagazine.com/technical/szabos-micropayments-and-mental-transaction-costs-25-years-later)
- **Chiou & Tucker, "Paywalls and the demand for news" (*Information Economics and Policy*, 2013).** In a media publisher's field test of paywalls across local markets, visits fell **51%** after the paywall was introduced, with a steeper decline among younger readers (taken from summaries of the paper). — [Chiou & Tucker (PDF)](https://www.oxy.edu/sites/default/files/assets/Economics/Chiou/chiou_and_tucker_paywalls.pdf); [MIT DSpace record](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/110528)
- **Other paywall data points (2010–2011).** NYT's metered paywall, which kept a free allowance, cut most days' visits by 5–15% in the 12 days after launch (Experian Hitwise). The Times (UK) hard paywall cut visits by about two-thirds in the following weeks. — [Search Engine Watch (2011)](https://searchenginewatch.com/2011/04/12/hitwise-reports-paywall-causes-decrease-in-overall-visits-to-nytimes-com); [Benton (2010)](https://www.benton.org/headlines/times-website-visits-fall-two-thirds)
- **Blendle (pay-per-article news, launched 2014).** By 2017, of 1M+ registered users, only **150,000 had ever made a micropayment**. Many used only the free starter credits. In 2019 the company pivoted to a €10/month premium subscription. The co-founder said "quarters per article are not going to make the difference," and premium members spent 22 minutes a day, three times as much as per-item buyers. The German and US per-article services closed in 2023, citing a "very limited user base." — [Nieman Lab (2019)](https://www.niemanlab.org/2019/06/micropayments-for-news-pioneer-blendle-is-pivoting-from-micropayments/); [Nieman Lab (2023)](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/08/the-poster-child-for-micropayments-for-news-is-getting-out-of-the-micropayments-business/); [journalism.co.uk](https://www.journalism.co.uk/blendle-shuts-down-micropayment-model-due-to-very-limited-user-base/)
- **Pauwels & Weiss (2008), "Moving from Free to Fee," *Journal of Marketing*.** One online content provider studied. Long-term revenue losses came mainly from fewer new free subscribers and from search-engine referrals and emails becoming less effective at bringing in users. Growth in free subscribers before the switch helped it succeed. Emails pushing free users to pay raised subscription revenue but cut advertising revenue. (Abstract only.) — [marketingandmetrics.com abstract](https://marketingandmetrics.com/moving-from-free-to-fee-how-online-firms-market-to-successfully-change-the-business-model)

### Inferences
- In this evidence it is the boundary at zero that does most of the damage. Expect a $1 price to lose nearly as many leads as $5 while earning far less. At $1, Stripe's fixed 30¢ also takes about 33% (see Q6). If Ask Linc tests a price, low single dollars do not buy back much volume.
- The calculators' acquisition engine (SEO for "free retirement calculator," ad copy, email sharing) depends on the free signal. Pauwels & Weiss found free-to-fee switches weaken search and email channels specifically. That cost would not appear in a short on-page conversion test.
- The best analogs (paywall −51% visits; Blendle about 15% of registered users ever paying, even with free starting credits; opt-out vs opt-in trial signup gaps in Q5) suggest a payment gate would cut completed captures by well over half. That is an inference from analogs, not a measured figure for calculators.
- Ask Linc's current design already holds the result behind an email gate. A $5 charge would add Szabo-style decision cost (is this worth $5? will I be charged again?) on top of the email decision.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed or reputable practitioner measurement of free → $1–$5 conversion for a web calculator or similar one-shot tool.
- I could not verify Josh Kopelman's "penny gap" claim with a source, so it is not cited.
- The Chiou & Tucker figure comes from summaries; I did not read the paper's tables.

## 2. Pay-what-you-want (PWYW): average payment, participation, long-run sustainability, and the effect of minimums and suggested prices

### Takeaway
PWYW reliably raises participation and lowers the average price. Revenue goes up only under particular conditions. The successes are face-to-face or community settings (restaurants, a theme park with a charity tie-in), or have a floor plus anchors (Lunch Money's $40–$150 annual range). Anonymous online PWYW with a $0 floor attracts mostly free-riders: about 62% of *In Rainbows* downloaders paid nothing, and buyers who were paying anyway averaged about $0.92 on a $12.95 photo. Suggested prices pull payments toward the anchor. Minimum prices help in theory but may deter some buyers. I found no pooled effect-size meta-analysis.

### Cited Findings
- **Kim, Natter & Spann (2009), "Pay What You Want: A New Participative Pricing Mechanism," *Journal of Marketing* 73(1):44–58.** The abstract notes that sellers risk buyers "paying nothing at all or a price below the seller's costs." — [LMU ePub record](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/92068/)
- **Frankfurt field experiments (2007), almost certainly the KNS data.** At a Persian restaurant's lunch buffet over two weeks in Nov–Dec 2007, the average price fell from **€7.99 to €6.44 (−19%)**, units sold rose from **157 to 253 (+61%)**, and revenue rose from **€1,254 to €1,629 (+30%)**. Snarkmarket separately reported "20% less" paid on average, traffic up 30%, and a net revenue gain. The link to the KNS paper is inferred; the secondary sources do not name the authors. — [Wiglaf Journal](https://wiglafjournal.com/if-a-business-lets-you-pay-what-you-want-could-it-survive); [Snarkmarket (2009)](https://snarkmarket.com/blog/snarkives/briefly_noted/pay_what_you_want/)
- **Gneezy, Gneezy, Nelson & Brown (2010), *Science* 329:325–327.** Over 113,000 theme-park riders were offered a souvenir photo under four conditions:

  | Condition | Purchase rate | Average price paid |
  |---|---|---|
  | Fixed $12.95 | **0.50%** | $12.95 |
  | Fixed $12.95, half to charity | **0.59%** | $12.95 |
  | PWYW | **8.39%** | **$0.92** |
  | PWYW, half to charity | **4.49%** | **$5.33** |

  Coverage says the last condition produced "significant profits." — [Freakonomics summary](https://freakonomics.com/2010/07/how-to-maximize-pay-what-you-wish-pricing); [Wikipedia: Ayelet Gneezy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayelet_Gneezy)
- **Riener & Traxler (2012), "Norms, moods, and free lunch," *J. Socio-Economics* 41(4):476–483.** Two years of data from a PWYW restaurant. "The vast majority of guests makes strictly positive payments." Average payments "decline slightly, converging at a positive level." Daily guests rose steadily, "resulting in a considerable increase in total revenues." Weather-driven mood changes affected payments. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/soceco/v41y2012i4p476-483.html)
- **Radiohead, *In Rainbows* (Oct 2007, comScore panel estimate).** **62%** of downloaders paid nothing. The 38% who paid averaged about **$6** (US payers $8.05, non-US $4.64), a blended **~$2.26** per download. Radiohead disputed outside estimates and never released its own figures. — [comScore press release (2007)](https://www.comscore.com/Insights/Press-Releases/2007/11/Radiohead-Downloads); [InformationWeek](https://www.informationweek.com/it-infrastructure/radiohead-fans-ears-open-wallets-closed)
- **Humble Bundle (2010–2012 data, secondary).** Averages varied by bundle: over $9 for the original, generally $5+ for later ones, just over $4 for a December 2011 bundle, and $8.53 for Humble Indie Bundle 5. Bonus content for beating the average was used to lift payments. In 2021 Humble replaced free-form splits with a minimum 15–30% Humble share, citing rising costs, after a backlash over removing sliders. — [Techdirt (2010)](https://www.techdirt.com/2010/05/19/some-final-stats-on-the-humble-indie-bundle/); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/latest-humble-indie-bundle-nets-5-million); [GamingOnLinux (2021)](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2021/07/humble-bundle-confirms-changes-coming-mid-july-to-add-a-minimum-payment-for-humble/page=1/); [Kotaku (2021)](https://kotaku.com/despite-backlash-humble-bundles-donation-limit-returns-1847212248)
- **Lunch Money (personal-finance SaaS; founder blog, July 2024).** Summer 2023: annual plan moved from $100 to PWYW **$40–$150**, same features at every price, with gifts only for top payers.
  - About **37%** of users on the new pricing pay above $100/yr (including those who stay on the $10 monthly plan), and they account for **>60% of revenue**.
  - Only **10%** of legacy users switched to a lower price.
  - ARPU fell, but MRR "nearly doubled in the last 10 months." This is heavily confounded by Mint's shutdown, which sent users looking for a replacement.
  - An editor's note says plans have since changed.
  - — [Lunch Money blog](https://lunchmoney.app/blog/we-lowered-our-pricing)
- **Panera Cares (2010–2019, pay-what-you-can cafés).** The plan assumed 60% would pay full price, 20% more, and 20% less or nothing. Founder Ron Shaich said more than half paid the suggested price and 20% paid a premium. He also cited problems with staff and with customers who objected to the homeless people who used the cafés. Panera called the last café (Boston, closed Feb 2019) "no longer viable." Denver's nonprofit SAME Café has run a similar model for 13+ years. — [Boston Globe (2019)](https://www.bostonglobe.com/business/2019/02/07/why-didn-panera-social-experiment-pay-off/xLBOWbLo5VyndijDGQ1CyN/story.html); [NACS (2019)](https://www.convenience.org/Media/Daily/2019/Feb/8/4-Panera-Shutters-Pay-What-You-Can_Foodservice); [10News (SAME Café)](https://www.10news.com/news/national/can-businesses-sustain-with-pay-what-you-can-model-denver-cafe-proves-you-can)
- **Mattermark (2014, B2B data report with a $1 floor).** The average sale held near **$7**, with 800+ pre-orders totalling $5,840 (from a search snippet; I did not open the page). — [Mattermark](https://mattermark.com/results-from-our-pay-what-you-want-experiment-for-the-mattermark-startup-traction-report)
- **Gumroad (2026, third-party modelled, directional only).** Across 146,271 products, PWYW listings had about 8% more sales than fixed-price ones but an average price of $18 vs $66 (−65%). Buyers with no anchor paid near the minimum. This is modelled from ratings counts by a vendor selling data subscriptions. — [InsightRaider](https://insightraider.com/en/data/gumroad-statistics-2026)
- **Vimeo OTT PWYW beta (company data).** About a third of buyers chose to pay above the base price. — [Vimeo Help Center](https://help.vimeo.com/hc/en-us/articles/12427302948625-A-breakdown-of-Pay-What-You-Want-for-Vimeo-OTT)
- **Anchors and suggested prices.**
  - Johnson & Cui (2013, *J. Business Research*): minimum, maximum and suggested prices all act as anchors and pull chosen prices toward them. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0148296312002524)
  - Yen (2024, *Psychology & Marketing*): a higher suggested price increases payments. The effect is weaker when a charity element is present. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mar.21993)
- **Minimum prices: theory vs evidence.**
  - Chen, Koenigsberg & Zhang (2017, *Marketing Science*, analytical model): "requiring or suggesting a minimum price is a good way to improve the profitability" of pay-as-you-wish. — [LBS PDF](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/836/1/KoenigsbergO_PAYW%20Oct%202016_AAM.pdf); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v36y2017i5p780-791.html)
  - A 2023 paper, citing Johnson & Cui, says minimum prices can have negative effects and create disadvantageous inequity. — [Springer, *Review of Managerial Science* (2023)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11846-023-00678-1)
- **Pick-your-price menus vs open PWYW (EMAC 2025).** Under name-your-own-price or pick-your-price mechanisms, the share of consumers paying more than under PWYW ranged from about 10% to 84% across studies, while only single-digit percentages paid less. — [EMAC 2025 proceedings (PDF)](https://proceedings.emac-online.org/pdfs/A2025-125952.pdf)
- **No pooled meta-analysis.** A 2024 PRISMA systematic review of PWYW found only one review/meta-analytic-style study (58 real-world instances). A bibliometric study lists meta-analysis as future work. — [ScienceDirect PRISMA review (2024)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2444883424000251); [Springer bibliometric study (2023)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41272-022-00414-6)

### Inferences
- Revenue per rider in Gneezy et al. (my arithmetic: purchase rate × average price):
  - Fixed price: about $0.065
  - Plain PWYW: about $0.077
  - PWYW + charity: about $0.239 gross, or roughly $0.12 after giving half to charity
  - Plain PWYW roughly matched a fixed price only because the photo's marginal cost is near zero. Ask Linc's calculator has the same zero-marginal-cost property, so a PWYW-for-results test would most likely produce "more takers, near-zero dollars."
- The conditions that sustain PWYW are missing from a one-shot, anonymous web calculator: face-to-face payment (restaurants), community or fan loyalty (Radiohead, Humble), a charity frame (Gneezy), and an existing paid relationship with a floor (Lunch Money). Lunch Money is the closest analog (personal finance, anchors, $40 floor). It worked as a way to lower the floor on a subscription, not as a $0-floor lead magnet.
- If Ask Linc tests PWYW anyway, the evidence favors:
  - a non-zero minimum;
  - a visible suggested price (the anchor effect is robust);
  - preset buttons (pick-your-price) rather than an open field;
  - a possible charitable or "support a small team" frame.
- Two costs work against it. Choosing a price is itself an added decision cost (Szabo). And at a $1 minimum, card fees take about a third (see Q6).

### Gaps
- I could not access the full KNS 2009 paper, so the cinema and delicatessen results are not reported here.
- Panera Cares revenue and payment data were never published.
- The Humble Bundle averages are 2010–2012 and come from secondary sources.
- Magnatune PWYW data (Regner & Barria, 2009) is often cited (buyers averaging above the recommended price), but I could not retrieve a verifiable source in this session.
- I found no published PWYW data for a finance tool or calculator.

## 3. Time-boxed passes, one-time purchases and weekly subscriptions: real examples, prevalence, refund and churn issues

### Takeaway
Paid one-time or short-term access works where the need is episodic and the deliverable is concrete, for example a vehicle history report at $44.99. In mobile apps, auto-renewing weekly plans now account for about half of subscription revenue. Their long-run retention is very poor (about 3–5% still subscribed after a year), and they carry "fleeceware" baggage. In SaaS, the best-known $7/7-day paid trial (Ahrefs) was shut down because it was abused for one-off data extraction. That is the closest analog to a "$5 for a week of calculators" pass.

### Cited Findings
- **Ahrefs $7 / 7-day paid trial.**
  - CMO Tim Soulo (March 2024 talk, third-party transcript): "We even made a paid trial. It was $7 for seven days that got abused. We killed that off, so we don't even have a free trial anymore." — [Latka interview transcript](https://getlatka.com/interviews/ahrefs-tim-soulo-2024)
  - Reseller and affiliate sites (commercially interested, unverified) date the end to late 2020. They describe the abuse as one-time data extraction and multiple accounts used to repeat the trial, and say the price was later $1. — [Toolsurf](https://www.toolsurf.com/ahrefs-group-buy-2026-premium-seo-intelligence-for-0-99-27/)
  - The paid trial existed by about 2019 (a Freshworks summit listing: "Why Ahrefs has a 7-day paid trial"). Ahrefs now offers the free, scope-limited Ahrefs Webmaster Tools instead. — [Freshworks](https://freshworks.com/freshsales-crm/sales-summit/tim-soulo)
- **Why Ahrefs and Surfer SEO moved from free to paid trials (vendor blog, via search snippet; page returned 404 when fetched).** Free trials attracted users with no intent to buy, which strained support and servers. Paid trials reportedly kept revenue growing while freeing up support. — [Userpilot case study](https://userpilot.com/blog/?p=6076)
- **One-time report example: Carfax.** A single report costs **$44.99** (VinAudit, Dec 2024 and Oct 2025). Multi-report bundles exist, but sources disagree on sizes and prices. — [VinAudit](https://www.vinaudit.com/?p=11530)
- **RevenueCat, State of Subscription Apps 2025, weekly plans (blog published July 17, 2025).**
  - Share of users on weekly plans by category: Gaming 77%, Business 44.7%, Social & Lifestyle 40.9%, Photo & Video 38.8%, Utilities 36.1%; versus Education 5.3%, Travel 5.3%, Health & Fitness 4.5%.
  - Median weekly price is **$4.99** vs a median monthly price of **$6.68**, so weekly users pay about **3.23× more per month**.
  - Weekly subscribers still active after one year: **4.2% (2024) → 3.4% (2025)**.
  - — [RevenueCat: weekly subscriptions](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/weekly-subscriptions/)
- **RevenueCat SOSA 2025, other figures.** Nearly 30% of annual subscriptions are cancelled in the first month. 35% of apps mix subscriptions with consumables or lifetime purchases. Gigazine's summary of the report: fewer than 5% of weekly subscribers stay more than six months. — [RevenueCat SOSA 2025 launch](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/company/the-state-of-subscription-apps-2025-launch/); [Gigazine (2025)](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20250318-mobile-apps-subscriptions-revenue)
- **RevenueCat trial-length analysis (published Sept 28, 2026; 17,000+ apps; Aug 2025–Jul 2026).**
  - Weekly-plan trial-to-paid is **22.3%** for trials of 4 days or less and **24.3%** for 5–9 days, vs monthly at **39.6% / 45.9%**.
  - Weekly first renewal among payers: 35.9% with no trial, 57.9% for trials of 4 days or less, 65.9% for 5–9 days.
  - Only **11.8–14.4%** of weekly trial starters both paid and renewed once.
  - — [RevenueCat: free trial length](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/free-trial-length)
- **Adapty, State of In-App Subscriptions 2026 (16,000+ apps, $3B+ revenue, 2025 data).**
  - Weekly plans were **55.6% of app revenue** in 2025, and weekly plans "convert 2–7x better than annual plans" (install-to-paid).
  - A weekly plan with a trial lifts annual LTV from $7.40 to $54.50.
  - Refunds "can cost up to 14% of subscription revenue." Photo & Video has the highest trial refund rate: 6.4% globally, 14.1% in APAC.
  - One-time purchases (including consumables and lifetime) grew from **6.4% of revenue in 2023 to 10.3% in 2025**.
  - — [Adapty 2026 report](https://adapty.io/state-of-in-app-subscriptions-report/)
- **Adapty 2025 report.**
  - Weekly plans are nearly half of all in-app subscriptions.
  - **65% of weekly users cancel within 30 days, and 5% are active after a year.** Day-30 weekly retention rises from 23% to 42% with a trial.
  - Annual plans have the highest refund rate (4.2%); weekly plans have the lowest.
  - — [Adapty 2025 summary](https://adapty.io/blog/state-of-in-app-subscriptions-2025-in-10-minutes/); [App Developer Magazine](https://appdevelopermagazine.com/in-app-subscriptions-report-for-2025/)
- **"Fleeceware" (Avast, March 2021).**
  - 204 apps with more than 1B downloads and more than $400M in revenue (Sensor Tower estimates).
  - Typically a 3-day free trial followed by **$4–$12/week**; one app charged $66/week.
  - Reviews called them scams. Avast advised caution with any free trial shorter than a week.
  - — [Gen Digital / Avast press release](https://newsroom.gendigital.com/2021-03-24-Free-trial-for-Only-3,432-per-Year-Avast-Discovers-new-Fleeceware-Apps-on-the-Google-Play-and-Apple-App-Stores); [Threatpost](https://threatpost.com/fleeceware-apps-400m-revenue/165040/)
- **App Store cut, if a native app were ever involved.**
  - Apple's Small Business Program charges **15%** commission for developers under $1M in prior-year proceeds. — [Business Wire (2020)](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20201118005376/en/Apple-Announces-App-Store-Small-Business-Program)
  - On the US storefront, an April 2025 contempt ruling barred Apple from charging any commission on external purchase links. In Dec 2025 the Ninth Circuit sent the case back to the district court to set a "reasonable" rate. As of mid-2026 that was unresolved and before the Supreme Court. — [MediaNama (2025)](https://www.medianama.com/2025/05/223-us-court-permanent-injunction-apple-app-store-commissions-external-purchases/); [Neonpay (Dec 2025)](https://www.neonpay.com/blog/the-latest-epic-v.-apple-appeal-ruling-december-2025-what-changed-for-game-developers); [The Next Web (May 2026)](https://thenextweb.com/news/supreme-court-apple-epic-contempt-stay-denial)

### Inferences
- A one-off calculator run delivers its value in one session. A 7-day pass adds little beyond that first run. That is the use-once-and-leave profile that led Ahrefs to kill its $7 trial.
- A "pay per result" product (Carfax-style: buy one report) fits episodic need better than time-boxing. For Ask Linc, a better-fitting paid object might be a deliverable, such as a saved PDF retirement plan, rather than "a week of access."
- Mobile weekly plans earn a lot per payer but retain few people: about 3–5% are still subscribed at one year, and in RevenueCat's data weekly trial-to-paid is lower than monthly. An auto-renewing ~$5/week plan on a personal-finance brand risks the fleeceware association. A non-renewing pass avoids that but ends the relationship after $5.
- If a pass is tested, framing it as "try full Ask Linc for a week for $5" (a paid trial of the core product) has better precedent than "unlock the calculator for a week." But Ask Linc already gives a 30-day trial free, which would undercut a paid 7-day one.

## 4. Credits, points and usage-based consumer pricing: how consumer AI products use credits, comprehension, churn, "meter anxiety," breakage

### Takeaway
Credits spread rapidly in AI products in 2025–2026, mostly B2B and prosumer, because they tie price to variable compute cost. The consumer-side evidence is mostly complaints: unpredictable burn, unclear unit values, allowances that expire, top-ups costing more than the plan, and bill shock (Cursor issued refunds in July 2025). Academic work explains why. People prefer flat rates even when they overpay (the "taxi-meter effect"). Pay-per-use mismatches raise churn. Abstract currencies distort valuation, and points tend to be hoarded.

### Cited Findings
- **Kyle Poyar (Growth Unhinged, Sept 2025).**
  - Credit adopters include Adobe, Apollo, Asana, Atlassian, bolt.new, Clay, HubSpot, Google, Lovable, monday, Replit and v0.
  - Credit pricing "can be hard to predict, hard to manage, and feel like a black-box." In a Stage 2 Capital write-up of his talk, he calls credits "the bridge" to value-based pricing.
  - — [Growth Unhinged](https://kylepoyar.substack.com/p/ai-credit-pricing); [Stage 2 Capital](https://gtm.stage2.capital/p/credits-arent-a-strategy-rethinking)
- **Adoption data (via HubSpot's summary).** Poyar's State of B2B Monetization 2025 (240 companies): seat-based pricing fell from 21% to 15% and hybrid rose from 27% to 41% in 12 months. PricingSaaS: credit models grew 126% in 2025 among the top 500 AI/SaaS companies. — [HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/website/ai-credits-buyers-guide)
- **Cost concentration (secondhand from a talk).** The top 10% of users might drive 70–80% of AI costs, hence the advice to cap usage. — [Stage 2 Capital](https://gtm.stage2.capital/p/credits-arent-a-strategy-rethinking)
- **Cursor (June 16, 2025).** Cursor replaced request limits with a dollar-denominated usage pool ($20 of non-Auto usage on Pro).
  - Users ran out after a few prompts and had no real-time view of their balance.
  - The CEO's July 4, 2025 post "Clarifying our pricing" took "full responsibility" and refunded unexpected charges from June 16 to July 4.
  - (Secondary sources, including a competitor's blog.)
  - — [FinTech Weekly](https://www.fintechweekly.com/magazine/articles/cursor-pricing-change-user-backlash-refund); [HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/cursors-credit-based-plans-leave-developers-puzzled-frustrated)
- **Lovable.** Review roundups rank the credit model as the "#1 complaint by a wide margin." Users pay credits for the AI's own failed fixes ("endless loops"). Separate allowances were merged into one pool, and guides cite "I expected $25, I got $60" bill shock. (Third-party guides, some commercially interested.) — [eesel AI](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/lovable); [Banani](https://www.banani.co/blog/lovable-pricing-and-credits)
- **Midjourney.** Plans buy Fast GPU minutes, and unused Fast time resets monthly. The $10 Basic tier has no unlimited slow mode, so users stop or buy extra fast hours at $4 each. — [eesel AI: Midjourney pricing](https://eesel.ai/blog/midjourney-pricing)
- **Gamma.**
  - The free tier gets 400 credits that do not refresh: about 10 AI decks at about 40 credits each. Users reportedly thought the credits were monthly.
  - Top-ups can exceed the plan price.
  - A Gamma feedback post reports credits charged for generations the FAQ implied were included. Trustpilot averages 1.9/5, but on only 15 reviews.
  - — [eesel AI: Gamma pricing](https://www.eesel.ai/en/blog/gamma-pricing); [Gamma feedback board](https://ideas.gamma.app/ideas/p/onduidelijk-inconsistent-creditverbruik-op-plus-plan-bij-standaardmodel-generati)
- **Lambrecht & Skiera (2006), "Paying Too Much and Being Happy About It," *JMR* 43:212–223.**
  - The flat-rate bias (choosing a flat plan even when pay-per-use is cheaper) is more common and more persistent than the reverse bias. The paper attributes it to the insurance, **taxi-meter** and overestimation effects.
  - The flat-rate bias "does not significantly increase customer churn," while the pay-per-use bias "largely increases churn."
  - — [UCLA Anderson PDF](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/marketing/paying_too_much.pdf)
- **Hsee et al. (2003), "Medium Maximization," *JCR* 30(1):1–14.** Inserting a points "medium" between effort and outcome changes choices. In one study, about three-quarters chose a 6-minute task for vanilla ice cream. When the tasks paid 60 vs 100 points, more than half chose the 7-minute task for pistachio. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v30y2003i1p1-14.html); [AcaWiki summary](https://acawiki.org/Medium_maximization)
- **Related currency effects.**
  - Spiller & Ariely (2018) review: consumers "do not sufficiently discount the number of units of a currency by the value of each unit." — [UCLA PDF](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Spiller_Ariely_2018.pdf)
  - Raghubir & Srivastava (2008): shoppers spent more with scrip than with cash. — [NYU Stern](https://www.stern.nyu.edu/experience-stern/news/professor-priya-raghubir-s-joint-research-payment-options-and-spending-behavior-cited.md)
  - Köcher, *The Paradox of Points* (12 studies): high-magnitude point currencies make collecting points appealing but make people reluctant to spend them. — [MIT Press Bookstore listing](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9783658095420)

### Inferences
- The main reason companies use credits, aligning price with variable compute cost, does not apply to Ask Linc's calculators. They are deterministic, application-owned engines, with a model used only for the optional plain-language reading on one calculator mode. Marginal cost is near zero. Credits would add a meter, and the taxi-meter discomfort that comes with it, without a cost reason, on a trust-sensitive personal-finance product.
- Credits make more sense for Ask Linc's LLM questions, where cost does vary. Even there, the Cursor, Lovable and Gamma record argues for:
  - a visible, real-time balance;
  - plain units (runs or questions, not abstract points);
  - no silent expiry.
- Points tend to be hoarded (Köcher). A prepaid points balance could therefore reduce engagement, which works against the nurture purpose of the lead magnet.
- A "points budget" is better used as a free allowance (for example, three free calculator runs and then sign up) than as a paid currency.

### Gaps
- I found no consumer comprehension studies specific to AI credits, and no published breakage (unused-credit) rates for consumer AI products.
- I did not verify Perplexity's or Character.ai's credit or limit mechanics.
- Vendor prices for Lovable, Gamma and Midjourney differ across third-party guides and were not checked against the vendors' own pricing pages.

## 5. Freemium, free-trial and lead-magnet benchmarks; what a payment gate does to email-capture volume

### Takeaway
Typical benchmarks:
- **Freemium (B2B self-serve):** about 3–5% of signups convert to paid (good), 6–8% (great).
- **Opt-in trials (no card):** about 18–25% trial-to-paid, depending on the source.
- **Opt-out trials (card required):** about 49–60%, but from markedly fewer signups.

No source measures what happens to a lead magnet's email capture when you charge for it. Every analog (zero price, opt-out signup gaps, paywalls) points to a large drop. The higher-precedent structure is to keep capture free and present a paid offer after capture: the "tripwire" placement.

### Cited Findings
- **Lenny's Newsletter (Aug 1, 2023; 1,000+ products; surveyed with OpenView, Growth Unhinged and Pendo; mostly B2B).**

  | Model | Good | Great |
  |---|---|---|
  | Freemium, self-serve | **3–5%** | **6–8%** |
  | Freemium, sales-assisted | 5–7% | 10–15% |
  | Free trial | **8–12%** | **15–25%** |

  Conversion is the share of new accounts paying within 6 months. Signup rates were about 5% for free-trial products vs 9% for freemium (denominator unclear). — [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-free-to-paid-conversion)
- **First Page Sage (86 SaaS companies, Q1 2022–Q3 2025, via aggregators).** Opt-out trial-to-paid **48.8%** vs opt-in **18.2%**. Visitor-to-trial from organic traffic is **8.5% opt-in vs 2.5% opt-out**. — [Shno statistics roundup](https://www.shno.co/marketing-statistics/free-trial-conversion-statistics); [LeadSync](https://leadsync.me/blog/ditching-credit-card-requirements-for-free-trials/)
- **Aggregated claim (origin unclear).** Card-required trials get about 35 signups per 1,000 visitors vs 45 for opt-in, but generate almost 3× as many paying customers. — [Shno statistics roundup](https://www.shno.co/marketing-statistics/free-trial-conversion-statistics)
- **Visionary Marketing 2026 (84,200 trial signups, 38 SaaS accounts).** Opt-out 60.4% vs opt-in 25.2%. — [Visionary Marketing](https://visionary-marketing.co.uk/blog/saas-free-trial-conversion-statistics-2026)
- **Flint.** Median B2B SaaS trial-to-paid is 18.5%; freemium free-to-paid is 2.6%. — [Flint](https://www.flint.com/blog/b2b-saas-free-trial-conversion-rate-statistics)
- **ChartMogul/ProductLed (via Userpilot).** Credit-card trials convert about 30%. Userpilot itself says these benchmarks are "very far from being perfect studies." — [Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/free-trial-conversion-rate/)
- **RevenueCat SOSA 2026 (115,000+ apps, $16B+ revenue, 2025 data).** Median trial-to-paid is **42.5%** for trials of 17+ days vs **25.5%** for 4 days or less. — [RevenueCat SOSA 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps); [RevenueCat trial-length blog](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/free-trial-length); [TechCrunch (Mar 2026)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/10/ai-powered-apps-struggle-with-long-term-retention-new-report-shows)
- **Adapty 2026.**
  - Average install-to-paid: onboarding paywalls with trials 1.78% (highest); web paywalls 1.10% vs in-app paywalls 1.60%.
  - AI apps: install-to-trial 10.92% vs 5.31% for apps on average.
  - — [Adapty 2026 report](https://adapty.io/state-of-in-app-subscriptions-report/)
- **Tripwires: low-ticket offers after opt-in (vendor framing, weak data).**
  - systeme.io: "A 3-5% tripwire conversion on a $7 offer can recover most of the customer acquisition cost." It places the offer on the page after opt-in, so the email is not gated by payment. — [systeme.io glossary](https://systeme.io/glossary/tripwire/)
  - An undated case compilation: 9,926 sales of a $7 product in 30 days from a 113,636-person list (about 8.7%). — [Scribd document](https://www.scribd.com/doc/303859638/Ryan)
- **$1 paid trial vs free trial (no controlled published test found).**
  - Publishers in an undated Subscription Insider webinar: cheap paid trials brought better-qualified leads and higher trial-to-paid, but fewer signups (no figures). — [Subscription Insider](https://subscriptioninsider.com/article-type/news/1-trials-often-better-than-free-trials-for-paid-content-and-freemium-sites)
  - PayKickstart: a $1 trial will "likely bring far fewer sign-ups." — [PayKickstart](https://paykickstart.com/the-one-dollar-trial-pros-and-cons/)
- **Paywall analog.** Visits fell 51% after a paywall was introduced (Chiou & Tucker; see Q1). — [Chiou & Tucker (PDF)](https://www.oxy.edu/sites/default/files/assets/Economics/Chiou/chiou_and_tucker_paywalls.pdf)

### Inferences
- Ask Linc's funnel is: calculator → locked result → email → signup → 30-day no-card (opt-in) trial. The relevant benchmark for the trial step is opt-in trial-to-paid, about 18–25% in B2B SaaS data. Requiring a card at trial start is a separate lever, with far stronger evidence (about 2.5–3× higher trial-to-paid, fewer signups) than any evidence for $5 passes.
- A payment gate at the calculator changes the very top of the funnel. Volume collapses and intent rises. Using the opt-in vs opt-out organic visitor-to-trial figures (8.5% vs 2.5%) as a rough proxy for "free gate vs payment-information gate," a payment gate could cut captures by about 70%. That is an analogy, not a measurement.
- The evidence-consistent alternative is to leave the email capture free and test a $5 paid add-on on the post-capture step (tripwire placement). That measures willingness to pay without losing the lead.
- Break-even logic for charging at the gate (my framing):

  `paid takers × ~$4.55 net + paid takers' downstream subscription value` must exceed `lost leads × (lead → paying subscriber rate) × subscriber LTV`.

  With a typical subscription LTV, even a few lost converting leads outweigh dozens of $5 sales. The test therefore needs Ask Linc's own lead → paid conversion rate and LTV, which I did not research.

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring email-capture volume for a free vs paid lead magnet or calculator.
- I did not retrieve primary data from ProfitWell/Paddle, Recurly or ChartMogul. Their figures appear here only through aggregators that repeat each other (First Page Sage, ChartMogul via Userpilot, Shno, Flint, LeadSync). Those figures are not independent confirmations.
- Tripwire take-rate benchmarks are vendor assertions, with unclear denominators.

## 6. Economics of a ~$5 card charge: fees, refunds, chargebacks, card-testing risk, app-store cut

### Takeaway
At $5, Stripe's 2.9% + 30¢ takes about **8.9%** (about 10.4% on international cards). Refunds do not return the fee. A single lost dispute ($15 fee) wipes out the net from about 3.4 sales. More importantly, a public, unauthenticated low-ticket checkout on a high-traffic SEO page is the classic card-testing target, and Visa's VAMP thresholds and fees tightened in 2025–2026. Below about $3 the fixed fee dominates.

### Cited Findings
- **Stripe US standard pricing.**
  - Cards: **2.9% + 30¢** per successful domestic charge; **+1.5%** for international cards; **+1%** if currency conversion is needed.
  - Digital wallets (Apple Pay, Google Pay) and Link: card rate. Wallets are listed under cards; Link shows the card rate.
  - ACH Direct Debit: 0.8%, capped at $5.
  - Disputes: **$15 per dispute**, plus $15 to contest, which is refunded if you win.
  - Refunds: no refund fee, but "the payment processing… fees from the original transaction are not returned."
  - Radar: listed from $0.05 per screened transaction pay-as-you-go, or from $10/month.
  - Stripe Tax: 0.5% per transaction (no-code). Billing: 0.7% of volume pay-as-you-go.
  - — [Stripe pricing](https://stripe.com/pricing)
- **Stripe on card testing.**
  - Card testers "create small amount payments, which cardholders are less likely to notice and report."
  - Consequences: disputes, higher decline rates that hurt legitimate payments "even after card testing ceases," extra fees, possible enrolment in card-monitoring programmes, and polluted revenue data.
  - Mitigations: Checkout or Payment Element (which include CAPTCHA, rate limiters and ML models); requiring login or session validation before payment; rate limits; and Radar velocity rules.
  - — [Stripe Docs: card testing](https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/prevention/card-testing)
- **Visa Acquirer Monitoring Program (VAMP), 2025–2026.**
  - Ratio = (TC40 fraud reports + TC15 disputes) ÷ settled card-not-present transactions.
  - The "excessive" merchant threshold fell from 2.2% to **1.5% on April 1, 2026** (US, Canada, EU, AP). Monitoring applies from 1,500 combined events per month.
  - Enumeration (card-testing) trigger: **≥300,000 enumerated transactions per month with a ratio ≥20%**.
  - Fees: about **$8 per disputed or fraudulent transaction**. Enforcement began October 1, 2025.
  - (Vendor summaries; Visa's own fact sheet not retrieved.)
  - — [Justt (2026)](https://justt.ai/blog/visa-vamp-threshold-changes-april-2026/); [Chargeflow](https://www.chargeflow.io/blog/vamp-visa-acquirer-monitoring-program); [Dharma Merchant Services](https://dharmamerchantservices.com/blog/visa-vamp-2025/); [Redo](https://redo.com/resources/articles/chargebacks/vamp-2026-shopify)
- **Chargeback rates (vendor data).**
  - Chargeflow: digital goods and subscription services combined **0.54% (2024)**, up from 0.34% (2023), driven by unauthorized-purchase codes and friendly fraud. Average dispute values: $77 for digital goods, $69 for subscriptions. — [Chargeflow statistics](https://chargeflow.io/blog/chargeback-statistics-trends-costs-solutions)
  - Sift Q3 2025 all-industry average: 0.26% of transactions (via chargeback.io, which also quotes a conflicting 0.60%). — [chargeback.io](https://www.chargeback.io/es/blog/average-e-commerce-chargeback-rate)
  - Ethoca 2025 (via Justt): average digital-goods dispute value $65. — [Justt on Ethoca 2025](https://justt.ai/blog/ethocas-2025-state-of-chargebacks-report-takeaways/)
- **App store.** 15% under Apple's Small Business Program for in-app purchase. US link-outs currently carry no Apple commission pending remand (see Q3). This only matters if a native app sells the pass. — [Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20201118005376/en/Apple-Announces-App-Store-Small-Business-Program); [Neonpay](https://www.neonpay.com/blog/the-latest-epic-v.-apple-appeal-ruling-december-2025-what-changed-for-game-developers)

### Inferences
These are my calculations from Stripe's list prices.

- **Fee as a share of the charge:**

  | Charge | Domestic card fee | International card fee |
  |---|---|---|
  | $1 | $0.33 (32.9%) | 34.4% |
  | $3 | $0.39 (12.9%) | 14.4% |
  | **$5** | **$0.445 (8.9%)** | **$0.52 (10.4%)** |
  | $7 | $0.50 (7.2%) | 8.7% |
  | $10 | $0.59 (5.9%) | 7.4% |
  | $20 | $0.88 (4.4%) | 5.9% |

  Net on a domestic $5 sale is **$4.555**, or about $4.53 after Stripe Tax at 0.5%.
- **Refunds and disputes.**
  - A refunded $5 sale costs $0.445 out of pocket.
  - A lost dispute costs $15 plus the $0.445 non-returned fee, about **$15.45**: the net from about **3.4** successful $5 sales.
  - At Chargeflow's 0.54% digital-goods dispute rate, the expected dispute cost is about $0.08 per sale. All-in fixed friction is therefore about **$0.53 per $5 sale (~10.5–11%)**, before refunds, tax-compliance effort and any Radar fees.
- **Card testing.** A $5 checkout reachable by anonymous visitors from SEO or ad traffic is exactly what Stripe says card testers target. Mitigations:
  - put payment behind account creation or a signed-in session (Ask Linc's signup flow could do this);
  - use Stripe Checkout or Payment Element (built-in CAPTCHA);
  - add Radar velocity rules.

  An unchecked attack could push a small merchant's dispute and decline ratios toward VAMP thresholds and damage authorization rates for the core subscription business, which is on the same Stripe account.
- **PWYW with a low floor.** Fee drag is worst at $1 (about 33%). Any PWYW minimum should probably be at least $3–5.

### Gaps
- I did not verify whether Stripe offers micropayment-specific pricing. It has historically offered custom pricing on request.
- I found no refund rates specific to low-ticket one-time digital purchases. Adapty's refund data covers in-app subscriptions only.
- I did not retrieve Visa's primary VAMP fact sheet. The figures above come from vendor summaries, which disagree on RDR/CE3.0 exclusions and LAC thresholds.

## 7. How to run pricing experiments rigorously (price A/B, fake-door/painted-door, Van Westendorp, Gabor-Granger) and sample sizes for small-traffic sites

### Takeaway
Practitioner guidance is consistent:
- use **painted-door tests** (an honest reveal page) to measure click-intent;
- use **randomized live-price tests** or refundable pre-orders for real commitment;
- use **Van Westendorp or Gabor-Granger surveys** (about 200–300 completes, at least 100 per segment) for ranges.

For a small-traffic site the arithmetic matters. Detecting a large drop in email capture takes only a few hundred visitors per arm. Pinning down a 2–3% payment rate takes thousands. The downstream effect on subscriptions needs far more.

### Cited Findings
- **Fake-door / painted-door tests.**
  - Place a UI element that appears real (button, menu item, pricing CTA). The click leads to an honest reveal page saying the feature is in development or early access, ideally with a waitlist or a pricing question.
  - "Click-through rate measures curiosity more than commitment."
  - A pre-order that charges real money and refunds if the product does not ship is "the strongest commitment signal."
  - Randomizing price points across visitors gives revealed-preference data.
  - Do not fake "Pay" or "Cancel" buttons that users rely on.
  - — [Koji fake-door guide](https://www.koji.so/docs/fake-door-testing-guide); [Userpilot on fake-door testing](https://userpilot.com/blog/fake-door-testing/)
- **Survey sample sizes (vendor guidance; no single standard):**

  | Source | Guidance |
  |---|---|
  | QuestionPro | ≥100 completes for Van Westendorp or Gabor-Granger, ≥100 per compared segment |
  | Conjointly | ≥200 total, ≥100 per segment |
  | IntoTheMinds (Van Westendorp) | works from about 250, recommends ≥400; 200–300 often enough if representative |
  | Survalyzer | ≥200 for meaningful results |
  | Qualtrics | about 300 completes for a typical study |
  | SurveyMonkey | chart will not render below 30 responses (a technical floor, not a precision standard) |

  — [QuestionPro](https://www.questionpro.com/webinar/pricing-research-webinar-van-westendorp-gabor-granger.html); [Conjointly](https://conjointly.com/faq/guidance-on-sample-size/); [IntoTheMinds](https://www.intotheminds.com/blog/en/van-westendorp-pricing-study/); [Survalyzer](https://education.survalyzer.com/knowledge-base/van-westendorp-price-sensitivity-meter-psm-solution-template/); [Qualtrics](https://www.qualtrics.com/marketplace/vanwesterndorp-pricing-sensitivity-study/); [SurveyMonkey](https://www.surveymonkey.com/categories/templates/price-optimization-van-westendorp/)
- **Paid-trial test design advice (synthesis in a search summary).** Randomize at the signup page and keep the product and onboarding identical. Use paying customers and revenue per visitor over at least one billing cycle as primary metrics, and track refunds, chargebacks and 60–90 day retention. Segment by traffic source. — [PayKickstart](https://paykickstart.com/the-one-dollar-trial-pros-and-cons/); [Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/?p=6076)

### Inferences
- **Sample sizes** (my calculations: two-proportion z-test, α = 0.05 two-sided, 80% power):

  | Comparison | Visitors needed per arm |
  |---|---|
  | Email capture 20% vs 10% (halving) | **~196** |
  | Email capture 20% vs 15% | **~903** |
  | 10% vs 5% | ~432 |
  | Payer rate 5% vs 3% | **~1,503** |
  | Payer rate 3% vs 2% | **~3,822** |
  | Payer rate 2% vs 1% | ~2,315 |
  | 30% vs 5% (a near-certain collapse) | ~32 |

  Revenue-per-visitor and downstream trial-to-paid comparisons have higher variance and longer lags. They need much more traffic and at least the length of the 30-day trial plus one billing cycle.
- **A suggested staged design for a low-traffic site:**
  1. A painted-door "Unlock for $5" vs "Unlock free with email" split on calculator traffic. Measure clicks and captures, with an honest reveal and the result delivered free to everyone who clicks. A few hundred visitors per arm is enough to detect a large drop.
  2. Van Westendorp or Gabor-Granger with existing signed-up users or leads (200–300 completes) to size willingness to pay.
  3. Only then a live, randomized post-capture paid offer (tripwire placement) with real charges, so leads are not lost. The primary metric is total value per visitor: $ collected plus downstream trial starts and paid conversions.
  4. Segment by traffic source (organic, paid, email), because intent differs sharply.
- Run these as holdout tests against the current free flow. Fix the duration in advance (whole weeks, to cover day-of-week effects) and do not stop on an early spike. Watch fraud and dispute signals from day one of any live charge.
- Ethics and trust: a painted door on a finance brand should never collect card details for something that will not be delivered. Deliver the result to anyone who clicks "pay."

### Gaps
- I found no authoritative source on minimum traffic for live price A/B tests on small sites. The sample-size table is a standard power calculation, not a cited benchmark.
- I found no Gabor-Granger-specific sample-size derivation; sources treat it together with Van Westendorp.
- I found no published case of a finance or calculator site running a free-vs-paid lead-magnet test.
